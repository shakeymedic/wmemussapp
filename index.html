Probe Selection & Knobology

Choosing the right probe and optimising the image are the first steps to a successful scan. Click on a clinical application below to see the recommended probe and key considerations.

Phased Array (Cardiac)

Small footprint for intercostal access. Low frequency (1-5 MHz) for deep penetration. Essential for all cardiac views.

Curvilinear (Abdominal)

Wide, curved footprint. Low frequency (2-5 MHz) for excellent depth in abdominal scans. The workhorse for eFAST and AAA.

Linear (High-Frequency)

Flat footprint. High frequency (5-15 MHz) for high-resolution images of superficial structures. Ideal for nerves, vessels, and pleura.

Key Principles: Artefacts & Probe Movements

Acoustic Shadowing +
Caused by highly attenuating structures (bone, gallstones) blocking sound waves, creating a dark shadow deep to the structure. A key diagnostic clue for stones.
Posterior Enhancement +
Occurs when sound passes through a fluid-filled structure with low attenuation (e.g., bladder, cyst). The area behind appears artificially bright. Confirms a structure is cystic.
Reverberation (A-lines & B-lines) +
The basis of lung ultrasound. A-lines are horizontal artefacts in normal lung. B-lines are vertical "lung rocket" artefacts indicating interstitial fluid.
Mirror Image +
A strong reflector (e.g., diaphragm) creates a "ghost" image on the other side. The presence of a liver/spleen mirror image in the thorax is a normal finding that helps rule out effusion.
Probe Movements +
  • Sliding: Moving the probe to a new location.
  • Tilting (Fanning): Angling along the long axis.
  • Rocking: Angling along the short axis.
  • Rotating: Turning on a central axis.
  • Compression: Applying pressure (key for DVT).

Core Diagnostic Modalities

Mastery of these four modalities is essential for managing critically ill and injured patients. This section breaks down each scan into its key views, interpretation points, and clinical significance. Use the tabs to explore the different components of each examination.

Echo in Life Support (ELS) & Shock

+

A rapid, goal-directed exam to answer specific haemodynamic questions and identify reversible causes of shock and cardiac arrest (The 4 Ps: Pericardium, PE, Pump, Pre-load).

Parasternal Long Axis (PLAX)

Key view for LV function and posterior pericardial effusions. Probe left of sternum, marker to right shoulder. Assess if anterior mitral valve leaflet nearly touches the septum.

Parasternal Short Axis (PSAX)

Rotate 90° from PLAX. Key view for the "D-Sign" of RV strain (septum flattens), indicating massive PE. Assess LV concentric contraction.

Apical 4-Chamber (A4C)

Probe at PMI. Key view for comparing RV:LV size. An RV > LV (ratio > 1) is a critical sign of RV strain.

Subcostal & IVC Assessment

Often the best view in cardiac arrest. Assesses for effusion and cardiac activity. A small, collapsing IVC (<2.1cm, >50% collapse) suggests fluid responsiveness. A large, plethoric IVC suggests high CVP.

eFAST

+

A rapid survey to detect free fluid (haemoperitoneum, haemothorax) and pneumothorax in trauma. Remember to repeat the scan after interventions or changes in status.

RUQ (Hepatorenal)

Most sensitive view for free fluid. Look for an anechoic stripe in Morison's Pouch. Scan superiorly to assess for haemothorax (look for spine sign, loss of mirror image).

LUQ (Splenorenal)

Probe more posterior and superior. Look for fluid above the spleen and at the splenorenal interface. Remember "knuckles to the bed".

Pelvic

Use the bladder as a window. Look for fluid in the rectovesical (male) or rectouterine (female) pouch. Best done before catheterisation.

Anterior Lungs

Use linear probe if possible. Look for lung sliding at anterior chest wall (2nd/3rd intercostal space). Absence of sliding suggests pneumothorax. A "lung point" is diagnostic.

Popliteal Sciatic Block

Excellent for ankle and foot fractures. Blocks leg below the knee (except saphenous nerve distribution).

  • Anatomy: Tibial and Common Peroneal nerves lie within a common sheath, superficial to the popliteal vein and artery ("Pop on Top").
  • Target: Space between the two nerves, within the sheath, 5-10cm proximal to popliteal crease.
  • Approach: In-plane, lateral to medial.
  • Volume: 15-20 mL of LA.

Advanced & Niche Applications

Expanding your POCUS skillset beyond the core curriculum adds significant diagnostic capability. These applications can provide rapid, crucial information for specific clinical presentations.

Ocular Ultrasound

Evaluate acute vision changes when fundoscopy is impossible. Contraindicated in suspected open globe injury.

  • Retinal Detachment: Thick membrane tethered to optic disc.
  • PVD: Thinner membrane, floats freely ("washing machine").
  • Raised ICP: Optic Nerve Sheath Diameter (ONSD) > 5mm (measured 3mm behind retina).

DVT Assessment (2-Point)

A rapid "rule-in" test. A negative POCUS does not rule out DVT in high-risk patients.

  • Goal: Assess compressibility of Common Femoral Vein and Popliteal Vein.
  • Positive Finding: Vein is not fully compressible.
  • Locations: 1. Groin (CFV at saphenofemoral junction). 2. Popliteal fossa.

Soft Tissue & MSK

Directly alters management by differentiating cellulitis from abscess and confirming fractures.

  • Cellulitis: "Cobblestone" appearance.
  • Abscess: Defined, complex fluid collection (may have "squish sign").
  • Fracture: Cortical disruption or "step-off" deformity.
